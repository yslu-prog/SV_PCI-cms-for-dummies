Getting PCI DSS Certified: A Beginner's Guide

Levels, Process, Timeline and Cost

Updated November 2026 · By the Secure Vectors QSA Team

When an acquiring bank, partner or enterprise client asks for your PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance (AOC), start by confirming three things: your role, your level, and how you need to validate.

Based on PCI DSS v4.0.1, this guide answers the six questions people ask most when they first deal with PCI DSS compliance, and covers what you need to do every quarter and year after you receive your AOC. It uses a service provider facing its first QSA assessment as the main example and notes where merchants differ. For our assessment services, see PCI DSS Assessment & Certification.

Key takeaways

  • PCI DSS is the global standard for protecting payment card data; the current version is v4.0.1. It applies to any organization that stores, processes or transmits card numbers, or can affect their security.
  • How you validate depends on your level. Level 1 requires a Report on Compliance (ROC) and AOC (service providers must use a QSA); other levels mostly use a Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ). Under Mastercard rules, third-party processors, 3-D Secure service providers and staged digital wallet operators are Level 1 regardless of volume; payment facilitators become Level 1 above 300,000 transactions a year.
  • A first assessment typically takes 3–5 months. For a small to mid-sized service provider, QSA fees are about US$15,000–25,000, excluding travel.
  • After you receive your AOC, depending on your level and SAQ type, you run ASV scans every quarter, penetration tests every year, and reassess annually.

01What is PCI DSS?

PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) is the global standard for protecting cardholder data, developed and managed by the PCI Security Standards Council (PCI SSC).

PCI SSC was founded by American Express, Discover, JCB, Mastercard and Visa; UnionPay joined as a strategic member in 2020. Any merchant or service provider that handles these brands' cards must comply, regardless of size. The current version, v4.0.1, was published in June 2024.

The 12 PCI DSS requirements

The standard has 12 requirements grouped under 6 goals:

Build and maintain a secure network and systems

  • 1Install and maintain network security controls
  • 2Apply secure configurations to all system components

Protect account data

  • 3Protect stored account data
  • 4Protect cardholder data with strong cryptography during transmission over open, public networks

Maintain a vulnerability management program

  • 5Protect all systems and networks from malicious software
  • 6Develop and maintain secure systems and software

Implement strong access control measures

  • 7Restrict access by business need to know
  • 8Identify users and authenticate access
  • 9Restrict physical access to cardholder data

Regularly monitor and test networks

  • 10Log and monitor all access
  • 11Test security of systems and networks regularly

Maintain an information security policy

  • 12Support information security with organizational policies and programs

02Who needs PCI DSS? Working out your level and SAQ

Any organization that stores, processes or transmits card numbers, or can affect their security, must comply with PCI DSS. How you validate depends on your role and level.

※ Mastercard-specific rules

Under Mastercard rules, third-party processors (TPPs), 3-D Secure service providers, staged digital wallet operators (SDWOs) and token service providers (TSPs) are Level 1 regardless of volume; payment facilitators are Level 1 above 300,000 transactions a year. A Level 2 service provider with a confirmed account data compromise is reclassified as Level 1.

Mastercard Level 2 merchants completing SAQ A, A-EP or D must also engage a QSA or an ISA (an internal assessor trained by PCI SSC) for validation.

Your acquirer confirms your actual level.

Level 2 and below: choose your SAQ type

Merchants at Level 2 and below also need to choose an SAQ type based on how they accept payments. The number of questions varies a lot: SAQ A, for fully hosted payment pages, is the shortest; SAQ D, for merchants handling card numbers themselves, covers every requirement.

SAQ types at a glance

AFully outsourced payments

The online payment page is hosted, redirected or iframed by a payment provider; mail and telephone orders are fully outsourced.

A-EPWebsite affects payment security

Your site generates the payment fields and sends card numbers directly to the provider; the site itself does not receive card numbers.

BImprint or standalone dial-out terminals

Only imprint machines or standalone dial-out terminals; no electronic card storage.

B-IPStandalone IP-connected terminals

Only PCI-approved standalone terminals connected via IP; no electronic card storage.

C-VTWeb-based virtual terminal

Card numbers keyed in one at a time through a provider's web-based virtual terminal; no electronic card storage.

CInternet-connected payment application

A payment or POS application connected to the internet; no electronic card storage.

P2PEPoint-to-point encryption

A PCI-listed P2PE solution; no electronic card storage.

SPoCPhone or tablet with a secure card reader

A PCI-listed SPoC solution, where a phone or tablet is paired with a secure card reader, with all controls in the provider's user guide in place.

Note: Tap-to-phone acceptance without an external reader (MPoC) falls under a separate PCI standard and is not covered by SAQ SPoC. Ask your acquirer which SAQ applies.

DMerchants

Merchants that do not fit any of the above, for example those that store card numbers.

DService providers

For Level 2 service providers.

To find the SAQ that fits your payment setup, read Which PCI DSS SAQ type applies to you? or complete it online with SAQ DIY.

Two common misconceptions

  • "We don't store card numbers, so it doesn't apply." Processing or transmitting card numbers is also in scope. Systems that can affect card data security, such as admin hosts, authentication servers and log servers, may need to be included.
  • "Level 2 service providers only need to self-assess." The card brands' minimum is an SAQ, but acquirers or enterprise clients can require a QSA-signed ROC and AOC by contract. Check with them first.

03Who assesses you? QSAs, ASVs and acquirers

PCI DSS assessments are performed by Qualified Security Assessors (QSAs) approved by PCI SSC. A QSA company employs QSAs and can provide both consulting and the formal assessment.

RoleResponsibility
QSAQualified Security AssessorPerforms the assessment and issues the ROC and AOC
QSA companyQSACEmploys QSAs; provides gap analysis, consulting and formal assessments ※
ASVApproved Scanning VendorRuns external vulnerability scans, which you must complete every quarter
AcquirerAcquiring bankTells you your level, how to validate and when to submit your AOC

※ A QSA cannot assess controls they helped design or implement; a different, uninvolved QSA must perform the assessment.

Service providers have additional requirements, such as quarterly reviews confirming staff follow security policies (Requirement 12.4.2). See How to meet PCI DSS v4.0 Requirement 12.4.2.

5 things to check when choosing a QSA company

  1. Qualification: confirm on the PCI SSC website that the company is a listed QSA company covering the regions where you operate.
  2. ASV status: using one company for quarterly scans and the annual assessment keeps coordination simple.
  3. Service provider experience: ask for examples from businesses like yours.
  4. Local support: documentation in your language, onsite assessment and ongoing communication.
  5. Transparent quotes: clear assessment days, sampling approach and report timeline.

04The PCI DSS certification process in 8 steps

A PCI DSS assessment (audit) has two phases: preparation and remediation, then the QSA assessment and report. Click a step for details.

Phase 1: Prepare and remediateabout 2–3 months
1Define scopeMap card data flows

Map how card numbers flow through your systems and confirm which systems are in scope. The clearer the scope, the fewer systems you need to address.

2Gap analysisCheck all 12 requirements

Compare your environment with each of the 12 requirements, identify gaps and prioritize fixes.

3RemediateArchitecture, tools, policies

Adjust system and network architecture, add any security tools you need, and write or update policies and procedures.

4Technical testingScan, test and fix

Run vulnerability scans (including ASV), penetration tests, wireless scans, card data discovery and code review, and fix what you find. Further reading: Vulnerability scans are not penetration tests, 6.4.3 and 11.6.1: payment page scripts.

Phase 2: Assess and reportabout 1–1.5 months
5Gather evidenceConfigs, logs, records

Prepare system configurations, logs, records and training evidence. See also File integrity monitoring and PCI DSS.

6Internal pre-checkSelf-review before the audit

Review everything once more before the formal assessment to confirm all fixes are complete.

7QSA onsite assessmentInterviews, sampling, checks

The QSA uses interviews, sampling and configuration checks to confirm practice matches documentation. Small to mid-sized service providers typically need 3–5 days.

8Report and submitROC and AOC to your acquirer

The QSA issues the ROC and AOC, which you submit to your acquirer or the card brands.

05How long does PCI DSS certification take?

A first assessment takes about 3–5 months from kickoff to AOC.

Preparation1–2 months
Documentation~1 month
QSA onsite3–5 days
Reporting0.5–1 month

The timeline depends mostly on scope size, whether architecture changes are needed, equipment lead times, how many rounds of fixes testing requires, and how much internal staff time you can commit.

Which situation are you in?

A service provider's first assessment

About 3–5 months

Architecture changes and fixing test findings take the most time. The earlier you finish scoping and gap analysis, the smoother the rest goes.

Reduced scope

Usually faster than a first assessment

For example, if a payment provider hosts your payment page or tokens replace card numbers, fewer systems need attention.

Annual reassessment

Usually faster than a first assessment

If last year's fixes are complete, your architecture hasn't changed much and you've kept records of quarterly scans and annual tests, reassessment goes much more smoothly.

06How much does PCI DSS certification cost?

For a small to mid-sized service provider's first assessment, QSA fees are about US$15,000–25,000, excluding travel. You may also need to budget for system, security tool and testing improvements.

Costs you may need to add

ASystems

Functions with different security needs, such as web, application and database servers, may need to run on separate hosts (Requirement 2.2.3). You may also need time sync (NTP), file integrity monitoring (FIM) and log servers. Virtual machines are fine.

Indicative: 5–6 additional servers, about US$15,000 one-time.

Firewalls, intrusion prevention (IPS), intrusion detection (IDS) and web application firewalls (WAF).

Indicative: mid-range appliances from about US$10,000–16,000 one-time; a cloud WAF instead costs about US$1,500–10,000 a year.

CData encryption

For example, a hardware security module (HSM), or software-based encryption instead.

Indicative: HSMs up to about US$30,000; prices vary widely.

DStaff training

Security awareness, secure development and incident response exercises. If your own staff run vulnerability scans or penetration tests, they also need appropriate training.

Indicative: about US$600–1,600 a year.

ETechnical testing

Vulnerability scans (including ASV), penetration tests, wireless scans, card data discovery and code review. See technical testing and buy ASV scans online.

Indicative: about US$16,000–29,000 a year depending on the number of servers and applications; trained internal staff can handle some items.

FCard brand registration

Service providers may need to register with the Visa Global Registry or Mastercard SDP.

Indicative: about US$3,000–5,000 a year. Visa charges its registration fee to the acquirer, which may pass it on to you; Mastercard fees are separate.

What drives QSA assessment fees

  1. System complexity: number of hosts, operating systems, installed components and security configurations.
  2. Security devices and network segments: more devices and more complex segmentation mean more configurations, updates, access rights and logs to review.
  3. Connections to acquirers and service providers: more connections mean more complex data flows.
  4. How card data is stored and encrypted: more storage methods mean more items to check.
  5. Number of locations: more stores, data centers and offices mean more assessment days; backup data centers holding card data are also in scope.

Fees also vary by region, because PCI SSC annual fees and QSA labor costs differ. Actual quotes are based on the factors above.

Related standards

If your business also includes 3-D Secure (ACS, DS, 3DS Server), PCI 3DS applies; organizations handling PINs fall under PCI PIN Security. Both can be assessed on the same schedule as PCI DSS.

Local notes: Singapore, the US and the EU

PCI DSS requirements are the same worldwide. What differs is who asks for compliance, which locations fall in scope, and which local regulations apply alongside it.

Singapore

  • Who asks: PCI DSS is required by the card brands and acquirers.
  • MAS requirements run in parallel: since 10 May 2024, payment service licensees are subject to MAS Notice FSM-N13 on Technology Risk Management and FSM-N14 on Cyber Hygiene, alongside the MAS Technology Risk Management Guidelines. PCI DSS assessment and testing records can support these obligations but do not replace them.
  • Regional operations: if development, customer support or data processing sits in another country and can affect card data security, those sites are in scope and may require sampling or onsite assessment.
  • Onsite assessment: Secure Vectors' QSA services are available worldwide, including onsite assessments in Singapore.

United States and European Union

  • United States: there is no federal PCI DSS law; compliance is driven by card brand rules and acquirer contracts. Some states go further — Nevada (NRS 603A.215), for example, requires data collectors doing business in the state that accept payment cards to comply with the current version of PCI DSS.
  • European Union: PCI DSS sits alongside the GDPR, whose Article 32 requires appropriate technical and organizational security measures. Since 17 January 2025, financial entities including payment institutions are also subject to the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA). PCI DSS evidence can support both, but does not replace them.
  • Cross-border scope: sites in other countries that can affect card data security are in scope. Where card data, logs and assessment evidence are stored also determines whether they can be reviewed remotely, so confirm this during scoping.

FAQ

Q: If a payment provider hosts our payment page, do we still need PCI DSS?

Yes. Outsourcing can greatly reduce your scope, but merchants usually still need to complete the applicable SAQ and submit an AOC every year. Which SAQ applies depends on how the payment page is embedded — see Which PCI DSS SAQ type applies to you?

Q: How long is a PCI DSS AOC valid?

PCI DSS is validated on a 12-month cycle. You need to reassess and submit a new AOC every year. Submission timing is set by your acquirer, the card brands, or your clients.

Q: Can the same QSA company provide consulting and perform the formal assessment?

Yes. Under PCI SSC rules, a QSA who designed or implemented a control cannot assess that control. A different QSA at the same company who was not involved may perform the assessment, and the QSA company must document the separation of duties.

Q: How is PCI DSS different from ISO 27001?

ISO 27001 certifies an information security management system whose scope the organization defines. PCI DSS focuses specifically on cardholder data, with more prescriptive requirements and fixed-frequency technical testing such as quarterly scans. Organizations certified to ISO 27001 can reuse some policies and processes.

Glossary

CDE (cardholder data environment)
The people, processes and systems that store, process or transmit card data.
PAN (primary account number)
The payment card number.
Segmentation
Separating the CDE from other networks to reduce assessment scope.
Tokenization
Replacing card numbers with tokens so real card numbers no longer appear in your systems.
HSM (hardware security module)
A dedicated device for key management and encryption.
FIM (file integrity monitoring)
Detects unauthorized changes to critical files.
